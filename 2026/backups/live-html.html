Elections in Boulder, in the state of Colorado and across the country have the context of a failed war, policies that enrich the few at the expense of the many, and a general feeling of state and local governments dealing with the effects of an immoral and incompetent federal government run by Republicans.

My hope for 2026 is that Colorado and Boulder can keep their heads down, doing the critical work that needs to be done. Although it’s a common belief in some circles (Boulder’s Nextdoor) that students greatly affect city elections, the turnout rate from CU students in off-years has typically ranged in the 15-25% (with 2025 as the last reference).

That year, the US ratified the 26th amendment and voters aged 18, 19, and 20 were able to vote for the first time. That election saw the first Black resident elected to Boulder City Council (Pennfield Tate, who was selected as mayor), four incumbents unseated, and the city passed a charter amendment to limit building heights to 55 feet (Boulder County History: ’71 election unseated incumbents, brought height limit - Boulder Daily Camera).

Do I expect the 2026 election to be so disruptive to the status quo? No. But it’s certainly going to be different.

Aquiles La Grave

Aquiles La Grave grew up in Boulder. While he has not been directly involved in city politics before last year, I had a number of questions about his candidacy and had wanted to share what I have researched.

In 2012, La Grave was living in Louisville, Colorado and became involved in a local housing issue. La Grave was a founding member of the Louisville Citizens’ Action Council, which formed the year prior to oppose a planned set of apartments at the Safeway on South Boulder Road. In the article “The deal is dead: Louisville evaluates its prospects at Safeway site” in June 2012: 

Aquiles La Grave, a 10-year resident whose home is just north of the Village Square shopping center, said he and his neighbors want more than anything to see something replace the 55,000-square-foot building that housed the Safeway for 30 years. But it has to be the right project.

“We want to make sure that what goes in there doesn’t degrade our quality of life,” he said. “An empty Safeway is not impacting our neighborhood in any way except aesthetically — so we can be patient.”

The opposition delayed re-development of the empty shopping center by 12+ months, reducing the amount of housing proposed by nearly 40%.Through the process, the plan also included an Alfalfa’s grocery store at the reduced-housing site (Developer, Alfalfa’s president present Safeway redevelopment plan):

Faun Ellwood-Digel, who has lived in Louisville for 15 years, said she would prefer to see an even more scaled back development of the apartment complex — fewer than 100 units — but bringing in the grocer makes for a “reasonable tradeoff” for upwards of 120 units.

As you may know, Alfalfa’s closed that location and its business entirely in 2021. Unfortunately, either the reduced housing or the closure of the grocery store were good outcomes from Louisville.

For reference, Boulder’s Alpine Balsam project will have both a consolidation of city office buildings as well as significant housing (144 city subsidized affordable units — which as far as I understand would be the largest single development of affordable units yet in Boulder). La Grave’s accusations that that city may have misused affordable housing funds on this site are misinformation that undermine confidence in the city’s ability to complete the buildout of the city offices as well as provide this housing that Boulder certainly needs.

In September, I saw another concerning post from La Grave on Nextdoor. I referenced earlier that the Boulder Valley School District put out a plan in late August to potentially close several schools in the Boulder area due to declining enrollment. La Grave’s “just asking questions” post on Nextdoor insinuates that the school district may be intending to close Flatirons elementary such that it could build residential housing on a school property that would not be subject to local zoning.

A post like this is simply intended to scare voters on Nextdoor who have NIMBY-ish tendencies and would certainly find such a plan concerning — at a time when everyone is rightly concerned about school closings. However, BVSD rarely divests in school properties (the last time was Washington School in 2009 which was sold to a co-housing developer) and such discussion was not part of any school closure plan.

Lastly, in the past year, La Grave has been involved with redevelopment of the Boulder County North Broadway Complex attached to the Iris Ballfields. La Grave has been involved with the “Save Iris Ballfields” Committee with the intent to have the ballfields remain as part of any redevelopment proposal there.

  • Use: Housing, including an estimated 200+ affordable units 
  • Would it preserve the ball fields?: No

  • Use: Senior living community
  • Would it preserve the ball fields?: Yes

So, there are a number of factors here at play:

  • How much money Boulder County will make from the sale of property, which directly affects county budgets
  • How much housing will be built (and its relative affordability) on a site that supports lots of housing on a primary transit corridor
  • Whether or not the ballfields would be preserved (which many in the community find to be a community value)

Boulder County ultimately chose La Grave’s preferred proposal from The Academy, a senior housing developer. The opportunity costs for that move were a $17 million reduction from the county budget, and a greatly reduced amount and type of housing built at the site, while keeping the Iris Ballfields. Were there potentially other options that raised more money for Boulder County, built more housing, and still preserved the ballfields? Yes, quite possibly — but these were the options presented.

Whether or not you think that the City and County of Boulder should have preserved the Iris Ballfields (which certainly do have a community benefit), I have a lot of trouble with the consistent pattern in La Grave’s activism and campaign messaging. He’s running on a campaign of governance and financial stewardship, taking opportunities to lambast the city and staff over housing projects at Alpine Balsam and vehemently fighting against the City of Boulder 2K measure that would fund deferred maintenance on city buildings.

At the same time, Boulder still has significant affordable housing needs and budget challenges that I don’t believe La Grave’s approaches are suited to fixing. I believe his inexperience at dealing with the real issues that the city faces (particularly that he is running for mayor rather than for city council) are not going to help Boulder emerge strongly through this next chapter.

Lastly, La Grave recently made a post on October 2nd in which he undermines the City of Boulder’s excellent public matching funds program in order to make an attack on the current mayor. I’m a strong believer in public matching funds as a way to help level the playing field for candidates with less financial means, and I also used matching funds when I ran for Boulder City Council in 2017.

Per the City of Boulder, the limitations are as follows, in order to maintain integrity in our elections and not have them overrun by massive spending imbalances and corporate funding. It also aligns the city and candidates with good incentives for campaigns:

  • To accept only contributions from a natural person (no LLCs, non-profits, corporations, etc.).
  • To limit her or his expenditures to a predetermined expenditure limit. Candidate expenditures, expenditures by the candidate’s official campaign committee, and any coordinated expenditures with other persons all apply toward this expenditure limit. [Note: in 2026, this limit is approximately $25,000]
  • To contribute to her/his campaign no more than 20% of the expenditure limit from her/his own personal wealth. (This includes both monetary and non-monetary contributions.)
  • To return at least 50% of any unexpended funds to the city, but not more than the matching funds received.

In return, candidates also have a strong incentive to take matching funds: the city offers a one-to-one match for each donation. As donations are limited to $100 per person, campaigns with lots of grassroots support can definitely be competitive even if they don’t have wealthy donors.

I have a number of issues with this framing and such disingenuous attacks make it very difficult to trust a candidate.

  • Asserting that a candidate “took” money from taxpayers by using a city-sponsored system is a bad-faith attack.
  • The vast majority of candidates (and especially winning candidates) take public matching funds. The last significant candidate not to take matching funds was Bob Yates running for Mayor in 2023: (In the race for Boulder mayor, Councilmember Bob Yates has raised more money than all his rivals combined — Boulder Reporting Lab)
  • A significant limitation in taking matching funds is that the candidate cannot self-fund their own campaign more than a few thousand dollars. However, La Grave does not have that limitation, and would be free to spend tens of thousands of dollars on his own campaign in the last few weeks. Personally financing a campaign can have a significant effect on the outcome, as a CU Regent candidate spent over $100,000 of his own money in the June 2026 Primary, much of that in the final weeks of the race.
  • Notably, La Grave has already donated $10,000 to the Big Tent BoCo [Five Commissioners] initiative in 2026, so it is not unreasonable that he may consider additionally self-funding his campaign. When he posted above the graphic to Reddit, multiple people asked if he would abide by the spending limit that candidates with matching funds agree to. La Grave did not answer those questions while answering several others in the thread.

Amendment 82 (CONSTITUTIONAL): NO / AGAINST

Shall there be an amendment to the Colorado Constitution creating new law granting the right for consumers to purchase natural gas for cooking or heating in homes or businesses and for distributors and utilities to sell natural gas to consumers?

Who put the measure on the ballot?

Amendment 82 was placed on the ballot through a citizen initiative backed by operatives of Advance Colorado, a right-wing group supporting numerous measures on Colorado’s 2026 ballot.

Short Description:

Establishes a constitutional right for consumers to purchase natural gas for use in homes and businesses and a right for producers, distributors, and utilities to sell natural gas, subject to the measure's terms.

Analysis and Commentary:

Oppose. Amendment 82 was placed on the ballot through a citizen initiative backed by operatives of Advance Colorado, a right-wing group supporting numerous measures on Colorado’s 2026 ballot.

Amendment 82 is simple enough: putting a requirement that Coloradans use a particular energy source into the state constitution. Why should we require the use of any fuel, material, or otherwise in the state constitution? We shouldn’t.

Currently, natural gas is and will continue to be freely available in Colorado to consumers for purchase and businesses to sell. There’s no imminent state law, or city/county code aiming to threaten that. However, we do have a number of forward-thinking plans addressing how we might use natural gas and other fossil fuels in the future. In late 2025, the Colorado Public Utilities Commission approved such a plan:

The Public Utilities Commission approved a “Clean Heat Plan” that calls for the state’s natural gas utilities to cut emissions from delivering and burning natural gas by 41% in the next 10 years, and all natural gas emissions entirely by 2050. We talked to state regulators, clean energy officials and advocates, and consumer groups for answers to your most urgent questions.
Colorado plans to phase out natural gas heating in homes, prompting many reader questions. We have answers. — Colorado Sun

The reality is that with new advances in appliances, home batteries, and electrical power generation, moving to all-electric residential and commercial buildings will be cheaper, safer, and with fewer emissions. The plan recognizes that the state will need significant investment and subsidies to meet the goal. Even so, the 2050 target date does not propose any additional regulation on propane tanks or other natural gas uses. Within this plan, a full transition will take decades, which is why it’s important not to undermine such efforts by putting a mandate to use natural gas in the state constitution.

Living in Boulder, Amendment 82 would overturn local ordinances that the city has implemented to speed the transition. Since December 2024, building code updates now require all-electric construction for new or substantially-retrofitted buildings. Cities need to move ahead of the curve on new construction because a building built today may last 50 or 100 years with energy infrastructure investments that will be obsolete by that time. Even if you are a fierce defender of natural gas usage, the ordinance allowed reasonable exceptions for “commercial buildings, including those with commercial kitchens and laboratories,” according to Boulder Reporting Lab.

Ultimately, there’s no good reason to require the use of natural gas in the state constitution. Our state and local governments need to be vigilant in moving ahead on an energy transition, and ensuring that every person and family in Colorado has the means to economically transition our energy supply. If Amendment 82 were to pass, that means we will undermine our own energy goals, add sunken infrastructure costs over the next several decades, and ultimately have to undo a measure that would be a significant mistake.

Boulder County Ballot Question 200: NO POSITION

Shall the membership of the Boulder County Board of Commissioners be increased from a three member board to a five member board?

Who put the measure on the ballot?

No position. Boulder County Measure 200 was placed on the ballot through a citizen initiative backed by a group of more centrist Boulder County called “Big Tent Boulder.”

Short Description:

Expands the Boulder County Board of County Commissioners from three members to five. Organizers submitted 18,422 signatures; the Clerk issued a statement of sufficiency on August 5, and the commissioners certified the measure on August 25.

Analysis and Commentary:

I ultimately decided to go with “No position” on Measure 200, which is a first in the 10+ year history of Boulder Colorado Voter Guide. I have struggled with deciding my vote for many months, and tried to capture all of my thinking here. First I’ll address what I think are the most compelling reasons I found to vote No, and several compelling reasons for a Yes vote.

Compelling reasons to vote No:

  • Boulder County’s commissioners can move more quickly with only three commissioners. The roles have traditionally been less a legislative body and more managerial. Traditionally, commissioners had a staff and management component. Only since 2020 has the county had a county administrator (similar to a city manager) that staff reported to (and that person has recently resigned).
  • While many proponents state that having five commissioners as increasing accountability, I’m not sure how that argument makes sense. By nature, increasing the number of people making a decision would decrease the amount of accountability any one person has in the decision. Regardless of whether we move to a district model (which I oppose) or a three-district, two at-large model, I don’t see how you can argue it makes the system more accountable.
  • Moving to a five-district model would be a truly bad outcome. While the current model of three commissioners, all at-large, also has some negative aspects, having five districts would divide up the county awkwardly (cutting up three population areas into five parts) and removes the incentive to work together as a whole to solve the county’s challenges, and promotes the incentive to do what’s best for one’s own district. I write more about this on Boulder County Measure 201.
  • There’s also a cost to increasing the number of commissioners. While not my favorite argument, the reality is that adding two commissioners will increase the county budget by $400-500k, while at the same time Boulder County’s 2027 budget already has significant cuts due to decreased revenues, eliminating 36 positions which includes 10-15 layoffs. The increased cost might be justified if convincingly showed a tangible improvement in governance but I’m not sure that it will.
  • I’ve spoken with two people I respect greatly, former County Commissioners Will Toor and Elise Jones, who oppose the change, and outlined eight solid arguments against the change as well: Five current and former Boulder County commissioners: Don’t expand the commission to five seats — Boulder Reporting Lab
  • I don’t particularly trust the group Big Tent BoCo, which put the measure on the ballot. The group leans moderate to conservative as a whole, and I presume that the intention is to try to expand their influence in Boulder County. The group has accepted $20,000 from Boulder conservative / Trump voter Dan Caruso, as well as $10,000 from Aquiles La Grave, and $10,000 from Kent Thiry, one of the primary funders behind the narrowly-defeated 2024 ballot measure Proposition 131 which would have drastically changed Colorado’s primary and general election system. I like to judge measures based on their merits but like most everything on Colorado’s 2026 ballot, you also have to consider the source.

Now, the Yes arguments that I find compelling:

  • How we elect county commissioners has often been not well-contested in the past fifteen years. Because Boulder is a heavily Democratic county, the only potential competitive races are in the Democratic Primary. We had one closely contested race in 2020, one in 2022, and a fairly uneven race in 2012. I do think more races would be contested and open if we moved to five commissioners, but likely more so with the 3-district / 2 at large rather than the five district system.
  • The current commissioners don’t seem to discuss policy and work together well due to open meetings laws and requirements for transparency. A discussion between two commissioners about a policy item is problematic when that is also a quorum of the board. It’s possible that having five commissioners might improve communication.
  • Right now, a commission of three leads to a lot of bad incentives. I was having a conversation with my fellow Boulder Progressives board member Adrian Fine — Adrian was previously the Mayor of Palo Alto before moving to Boulder. He has a few insights from his time as an elected official that I’d like to share:
    • “With 3: if you're on the losing side, the majority knows that and the dynamics become multiple against singular. With a 4th or 5th vote, the majority cannot just be against 1 person; they have to earn/please/placate at least one more. So the extra vote prevents railroading behavior.”
    • If you're trying to assemble a winning coalition, it means it cannot just be you and one other, you are going to have to get a 3rd opinion. Your argument for or against something cannot just be tailored to the one, only other person that matters.
    • “And it's not just the elected officials. Staff has the exact same incentive; ‘oh we know the commission is split? let's build X report to persuade the middle’”
  • Currently, it may be more difficult to meet with the commissioners because there are few of them or work may be held up by the absence of a single commissioner.
  • While not a great argument for a systemic change, a lot of people have significant grievances and mistrust with the current set of commissioners. Some people like the idea of a fresh start with a larger group. However, that’s somewhat moot because two of the commissioners will be term-limited in 2028.

I tend to be pretty open-minded, and have been grappling with this commissioner question for the past 8+ months. I still don’t have a much better direction than when I first started thinking about the issue. I also find myself thinking about the City of Boulder’s move to directly elect its mayor in 2020 (with the first election in 2023). The measure was popular, I supported it with mixed feelings, and it passed with 78.14% of the vote. I understand the appeal of voting for mayor plus four council people compared to voting for five council people, but I am not sure it necessarily made our governance any better. We do not have a strong mayor system, and decisions are still made by five votes on council and working with the city manager to implement that vision.

The commissioners ballot measure certainly has trade-offs. I’m not certain that the trade-offs with five commissioners are better than the trade-offs of the current system. We will see what happens.

Boulder County Ballot Question 201: Option B

If the membership of the Boulder County Board of Commissioners is increased from a three member board to a five member board, by what method shall the Boulder County Board of Commissioners be elected? (Vote for One)

Who put the measure on the ballot?

Short Description:

A conditional question that only takes effect if Question 200 passes: should all five commissioners be elected by voters in their respective districts, or should three district-elected commissioners be joined by two commissioners elected countywide?

Analysis and Commentary:

Option B. Boulder County Measure 201 was placed on the ballot through a citizen initiative backed by a group of more centrist Boulder County called “Big Tent Boulder.”

I am voting for Option B. If Boulder County voters pass Boulder County measure 200, then Boulder County measure 201 will determine how moving to five commissioners will be implemented.

Possible Option A:

Should all five commissioners be elected by voters in their respective districts?

Above is a map of the current three Boulder County Commissioner districts. Here are a number of problems with creating five districts:

  • Boulder County has three population centers. It’s not clearly possible to map these three population centers onto five districts without interspersing districts and breaking up communities of interest.
  • Here’s a quick effort I made to create five districts using a helpful tool called Districtr. Here’s a link to my map. There are probably a few variations you could attempt here, but the typical district needs to be approximately 66k people, while Boulder itself is a little more than 100k in population and so is Longmont. So already, you likely have Boulder and Longmont more represented than other parts of the county, while breaking up those communities of interest. 
  • I wanted to bring in an argument from a piece against the county commissioner change:
    • Increasing the number of commissioners won’t provide greater representation for geographic groups that feel underrepresented. At just 46,000 total residents, there aren’t enough residents in all of unincorporated Boulder County to create a district dedicated just to them. Nor could there be a district dedicated to mountain residents, with the combined population of Nederland, Allenspark, Lyons, Jamestown, Gold Hill and Ward only about 5,000 people.
    • Five current and former Boulder County commissioners: Don’t expand the commission to five seats — Boulder Reporting Lab
  • Commissioners in five districts have incentives to be insular in their thinking — putting the needs of the people in their own district above the county’s needs as a whole. 
  • Single-winner districts means that the county may miss out on the service of good candidates because there can only be one winner per district. It also means that in some cycles, there may be a lack of good candidates in a particular district. These problems are mitigated by having both larger districts and at-large seats.
  • If you also think Option A is a bad outcome, you should really consider not voting in favor of Measure 200 to move to five commissioners.

Possible Option B:

Should three district-elected commissioners be joined by two commissioners elected countywide?

Here are my arguments for three districts, and two at-large:

  • For someone like me who is skeptical of the benefits of five commissioners, moving to three districts, two at-large would be a smaller change from the current system. The same existing districts could be used for the new system, which are relatively aligned with Boulder County’s population centers
  • Right now all Boulder County residents get to vote on all commissioners, three in total. In the five district system, each person would only get to vote on one commissioner, which is a significant downgrade in influence. In Option B, people would still get to vote for three of five commissioners — one in their own district, and two at-large
  • In having both districts and at-large, potential candidates can weigh which is a better fit for themselves based on their geographical location, experience in elected office, and relationships across the county.
  • When two strong candidates live in the same district, one may choose to run at-large. That can be a benefit to the county to have both as options for election. As I have mentioned, even with five districts, Boulder or Longmont would very likely have two people representing each city because these population centers would be split. With an at-large system, you might still get two commissioners from the same city, but they won’t be strictly eligible in one district based on a district barrier.

Support. City of Boulder Issue 2J was placed on the ballot by the Boulder City Council through Ordinance 8759.

  • Unoccupied homes do not contribute to the local economy through sales taxes, nor provide people to work in the local economy, and kids to attend our schools. Support. City of Boulder Issue 2K was placed on the ballot by the Boulder City Council through Ordinance 8758.

Boulder Issue 2K is the most controversial local issue on Boulder's 2026 ballot. What’s not controversial is that a number of city-owned buildings are in disrepair or near the end of their usable life. The controversial part is what do we pay to address the deferred maintenance and when?

I strongly support Issue 2K. I’ll outline the basics and how I got there. I also want to mention how our Boulder City Council, with an 8-0 vote, supported putting the bond on the ballot. Setting election politics aside, that’s a strong show of support from every council person across ideological lines.

  • What if we vote on this again in 2027 or 2028?

What does the bond do and why do we need it?

Boulder has a Facilities Master Plan which “provides a strategic direction for 75 buildings, with over 1.8 M square feet of space and a current replacement value of over $550M.” The Boulder Issue 2K Recreation and Safety Bond proposes “$400 million to pay for specific infrastructure projects” along with estimated costs and justifications for why buildings need replacement or serious remodeling.

  • South Boulder Recreation Center - $65,000,000
  • North Boulder Recreation Center - $72,000,000
  • West Age Well Center - (costs part of North Boulder Recreation Center)
  • Fire Station No. 1 - $5,000,000
  • Fire Station No. 5 - $2,000,000
  • Public Safety Center and 911 Dispatch - $170,000,000 (depends on selected location; this cost includes likely site infrastructure and construction of secure parking)
  • Municipal Services Center - $100,000,000
  • Penfield Tate II Municipal Building - $30,000,000

An important detail is that the city may stagger reconstruction burden and would not immediately bond for the full cost:

If the city bonded for the full $400 million, the owner of a property that is worth $1 million would pay an additional $400 a year in property taxes for 20 years. If the city bonds for less or staggers projects, the property tax impact would be less. It is likely that the city would sequence how much it borrows, so the full increase is unlikely to occur for a couple of years.

The other factor at play is: the city is passing a measure for the full bonding costs of facilities. However, because TABOR requires voters to pass such authorization, the city has an incentive to estimate the highest cost so that they don’t have to ask voters again for additional money. That’s not to say that voters should *expect* a lower total cost, but the city can and should take opportunities for cost reductions and alternatives where possible.

A good example is a recent proposal relayed from Boulder City Manager Nuria Rivera-Vandermyde. On September 24th, she emailed the Boulder City Council Hotline with updated information on the public safety building:

We have a late breaking opportunity to potentially lease and/or purchase an existing building in another location in the city that has the footprint needed to house PD operations. The potential cost of this acquisition and renovation would significantly reduce the costs needed to provide a future location for our police department’s future needs.

A number of people in progressive circles (including me) are not thrilled with Issue 2K’s allocation of funds, that roughly 42% of the bond funding would go to a new police station (as police operating revenue is approximately 10% of Boulder’s proposed 2027 budget). While such an alternative plan is still preliminary, the city could build a lot of good will by reducing the police station cost to balance spending over various needs. A new location may last Boulder another 50-75+ years:

The Public Safety Building, 1805 33rd Street, was built in 1968. It was the home of the Mountain Bell telephone company before the city purchased it in 1988 for about $2.7 million, according to county property records, and the police moved their operations there around 1989.

I won’t go over every project individually, but a number of the individual projects seem popular with the community: updating or reconstructing the South Boulder and North Boulder recreation centers, repairing and renovating fire stations 1 and 5, and creating a new / updated space for the West Age Well Center to continue support for Boulder’s aging demographics.

It’s also important to note that passing the bond does not simply allow each project to go forward without further discussion. Every major item will come back to Boulder City Council for public input and process, which is exactly what’s needed in order to make sure the city is delivering the right implementation of these projects.

What if we made the bond smaller?

Some people have criticized Boulder’s deferred maintenance bond as “too large.” The reality is that no one wants to pay for deferred maintenance. It’s not fun, it’s not sexy — it’s not anything other than a debt that in Boulder’s case, which hasn’t been paid or saved for in decades.

The City of Boulder polled on the measure in June and found the public split on whether to support the bond. However, a notable finding: a reduced bond paying for a smaller set of higher-priority buildings actually polled worse than the larger bond.

The city has outlined project start timelines based on priority, so a reduced bond would target those projects first:

Work on the city’s Public Safety Building relocation would begin first. The city has identified this building as having the greatest number of repair needs. This project would cost the most and would take the longest to complete. By starting soon, the city could limit cost increases that come with delays. If the city can add staff, it would be able to start planning other projects at the same time. The goal would be to finish all the projects within 10 years.
Recreation and Safety Bond — City of Boulder

So, if you are asking for a smaller bond, it’s going to be the public safety building first, and other priorities second. You might understand why that option would poll worse.

Even if the council brought back a smaller bond in 2027 and 2028, and miraculously passed it (which I’m skeptical about), then you have the problem of lumping less-popular projects together to pass future bond measures. That’s a bad outcome for the city.

In an opinion piece from former Boulder Representative and City council person KC Becker asking for more process on the bond, I wanted to highlight a comment from former Boulder City Councilperson Bob Yates. He later made a more inflammatory comment on Boulder Nextdoor that I think is instructive:

I have a couple issues with this framing. Part of it is what I already said — TABOR will give the incentives for the city to pass popular measures, when not every need the city has is popular. It’s notable that Bob said in his latest newsletter, “All I can say is, thank goodness for TABOR” when lodging his opposition to most taxes on this year’s ballot.

Secondly, Bob says that the committee “winnowed the staff’s wish list down from $700 million to $49 million” back in 2011. As an astute, longtime observer of Boulder politics mentioned to me: "Hmm, did going from $700M to $49M involve deferring any maintenance? I'll wait..."

Boulder’s problem with deferred maintenance is not new — the problem has been ignored and not paid for previously.

What if we vote on this again in 2027 or 2028?

If Boulder Issue 2K does not pass, and if Boulder comes up with a saleable smaller measure, what are the prospects of its passage in 2027 or 2028?

My professional opinion as part of my firm Colorado Community Research, which does ballot measure polling and political consulting, is that 2027 is dead in the water for a property tax measure. Off-year elections have a significant skew towards homeowners (closer to 75%, whereas city-wide is closer to 50%), as well as being more conservative. Given the currently mixed polling for an even-year electorate, trying to run a similar measure in an off-year would certainly fail. The only way to win such a measure is to make concessions to the loudest, most influential opponents of the measure currently (to gain their support), so that they are the deciders of how the city spends money, not the council or general public. That’s not a democratic outcome.

Running the measure in 2028 would delay the timeline another two years, and does increase uncertainty. The main risks are an economic downturn, which would again reduce the likelihood of passage. Another factor is that construction costs continue to rise faster than inflation, so projects would likely cost more by delaying them. Third, the city continues to pay for required and emergency deferred maintenance on these buildings out of the general fund, which continues to strain other parts of an already strained budget.

What happens if we don’t address the problem?

Beyond my concern that the city will continue spending a larger and larger part of the general fund on deferred maintenance, we are already seeing the effects of the City of Boulder’s decades-long negligence to deferred maintenance in what’s happening with our library budget.

The Boulder Public Library District formed after a 2022 vote, officially separating from the City of Boulder. As part of the formation, the City of Boulder has kept ownership of the buildings, but the library district has the responsibility to maintain them, including deferred maintenance. Now, due to a combination of state property tax cuts, lower than expected property tax values, and millions of dollars of deferred maintenance, the library district is now cutting 20% of its staff.

Additionally, the library buildings, which the district leases from the City of Boulder, are an average of 56 years old and require significant upkeep that has not been a major part of the district budget to date. Capital needs overall are expected to be higher in 2028 and 2029 because certain upgrades and necessary maintenance have been delayed and cannot be put off indefinitely. The current budget has little money allocated for facilities upkeep or future capital needs.

If Boulder voters need to understand how long-standing deferred maintenance can have a direct impact for ongoing operations and services, the challenges that the library district is working through is direct evidence.

What might that look like for Boulder? I’m not totally certain, but I can tell you that popular amenities like swimming pools and rec centers are more likely to be cut before funding for police officers. We’re already seeing a similar effect with Boulder’s Spruce Pool. While Boulder’s shortfalls in addressing public amenities like Spruce Pool are in operating costs rather than capital costs, if we do not address our capital funding shortfalls, they will continue to squeeze operations.

Is the cost for individuals and businesses too high?

I do think most of the pushback on the bond is about who pays and how much. I don’t take that lightly, and each voter will determine that for themselves. However, it’s fair to say that opposition is coming from wealthier voters, including Boulder conservative / Trump voter Dan Caruso.

As property taxes tend to be more progressive than not, I think it’s correct that voters owning more expensive properties should pay a higher share to address our infrastructure needs. It’s the correct position of the city to move toward property taxes to address these concerns rather than continuing to pay for long-term investments using regressive sales taxes. What will Boulder do this year? I’m looking forward to find out.

City of Boulder Ballot Question 2M: YES / FOR

Debt Limit City Charter Language Change
Shall Section 97 of the Boulder Home Rule Charter be amended pursuant to Ordinance 8762 to modify the City's debt limitation to be not more than three percent of the actual value of the taxable property within the City?

Who put the measure on the ballot?

City of Boulder Issue 2M was placed on the ballot by the Boulder City Council through Ordinance 8762.

Short Description:

Amends the city charter so Boulder's debt limit is calculated as no more than three percent of the actual value of taxable property rather than assessed value. The city asserts this is consistent with how most municipalities and the state calculate debt limitation.

Analysis and Commentary:

Support. City of Boulder Issue 2M was placed on the ballot by the Boulder City Council through Ordinance 8762.

The City of Boulder is looking to update its charter to allow a more reasonable amount of debt to be authorized in order to pursue future projects. Note that this ballot measure does not authorize any new debt itself, which must be authorized through a specific ballot question.

City Debt Limitation Charter Amendment (Attachment D - Ord. 8762) This measure would amend Charter Sec. 97 so that debt limitation could be no more than the “actual value” (rather than the “assessed value” as currently written) of the taxable property within the city. This amendment would bring the city’s charter in line with all other Boulder County communities and state statute. 

August 6th Agenda Packet, Boulder City Council

Boulder County has a useful terminology web page:

Actual Value is “That value determined by appropriate consideration of the cost approach, the market approach, and the income approach to appraisal.”

The assessed value is typically much lower, and determined by this formula: Assessed Value = Actual Value x Assessment Rate. The assessment rate is typically a fixed amount, i.e. 6.7% to determine what is taxable.

So in practice, the actual value is a significantly higher number and more reflective of a reasonable bonding amount — a maximum of 3% of the entire property value of all structures in the city.

Again, an important caveat — Measure 2M does not take on any new debt, and such debt must be authorized through other ballot measures.

The change to the city’s debt limitation would have no fiscal impact, but would allow the city to issue general obligation bonds above the current charter limitation of three percent of the assessed value of the taxable property within the city. Assessed value as of 2025 for Boulder is $5.0 billion. Based on the most recent assessed value, the city is limited to $150.7 million in general obligation debt.

Front Range Passenger Rail District Ballot Issue 7A: YES / FOR

SHALL FRONT RANGE PASSENGER RAIL DISTRICT TAXES BE INCREASED $295,000,000 ANNUALLY AND BY WHATEVER AMOUNTS ARE RAISED ANNUALLY THEREAFTER, AND SHALL FRONT RANGE PASSENGER RAIL DISTRICT DEBT BE INCREASED $580,000,000, WITH A REPAYMENT COST OF $785,000,000; TO CONSTRUCT, OPERATE, AND MAINTAIN COLORADO CONNECTOR (COCO) PASSENGER RAIL SERVICE ON COLORADO’S FRONT RANGE AND CONNECT COMMUNITIES, INCLUDING PUEBLO, COLORADO SPRINGS, STERLING RANCH, LITTLETON, DENVER, WESTMINSTER, BROOMFIELD, LOUISVILLE, BOULDER, LONGMONT, LOVELAND, AND FORT COLLINS; IN ORDER TO: REMOVE VEHICLES FROM HIGHWAYS AND INCREASE TRAVEL CAPACITY; CONNECT TRAVELERS TO EMPLOYMENT CENTERS, COLLEGES, SPORTS ARENAS, AND ENTERTAINMENT HUBS ALONG THE FRONT RANGE; AND INVEST IN STATION AREA IMPROVEMENTS AND LOCAL CONNECTIONS TO RAIL STATIONS; BY ESTABLISHING A 0.333% SALES AND USE TAX (EQUAL TO ONE THIRD OF A PENNY ON A $1 PURCHASE), WITH EXEMPTIONS PROVIDED UNDER COLORADO LAW, INCLUDING THOSE FOR GASOLINE, FOOD, RESIDENTIAL ELECTRICITY AND GAS, PRESCRIPTION DRUGS, AND MEDICAL SUPPLIES; AND TO RETAIN ALL SUCH REVENUES, PUBLIC AND PRIVATE CONTRIBUTIONS, AND ANY INVESTMENT INCOME ON REVENUES AND DEBT PROCEEDS, AS A VOTER-APPROVED REVENUE CHANGE UNDER SECTION 20 OF ARTICLE X OF THE COLORADO CONSTITUTION; AND REQUIRING THAT ALL SUCH DEDICATED REVENUES BE REVIEWED ANNUALLY BY AN INDEPENDENT AUDITOR AND A ROTATING GROUP OF CITIZEN TAXPAYERS WHO LIVE IN THE DISTRICT?

Who put the measure on the ballot?

Front Range Passenger Rail District Ballot Issue 7A was placed on the ballot with Democratic support through the state legislature through Senate Bill 26-172.

Short Description:

Would create a third-of-cent (0.333%) sales and use tax within the Front Range Passenger Rail District and authorize $580 million in debt to construct, operate, and maintain Colorado Connector passenger rail service between Pueblo and Denver, including Boulder and Fort Collins. Support. Issue 7A was placed on the ballot with Democratic support from the state legislature through Senate Bill 26-172.

Front Range Passenger Rail Ballot Issue 7A is Colorado’s best opportunity to establish true passenger rail extending along the north/south corridor. While there’s a lot to say about what’s on the 2026 ballot, Issue 7A, establishing the CoCo train is something that gets me truly excited about the future of Colorado.

What would Issue 7A do and why do we need it?

Issue 7A would institute a sales tax along the Front Range Passenger Rail district, which will serve as the primary funding source for passenger rail.

Here’s a quick fact sheet on the district:

  • 12 Front Range communities connected
  • 190 total track miles from Fort Collins to Pueblo
  • 2029 — Date that expanded service would begin
  • 15 new CoCo stations (13 Daily and 2 Special Event Stations)
  • Up to 10 round trips per day by 2045
  • 300-500 seats per train.

From an election perspective, state legislators made a smart choice to update the district boundaries before putting a measure to voters: “Under SB26-172, the district would be redrawn to a fraction of its current geographic size and shed about 40% of its population, shrinking to 2.6 million residents from 4.4 million,” per Colorado Newsline. The decision better aligns people paying the tax with people getting the most usage of the service, increasing the likelihood that the measure passes.

As far as service and number of trips, that is why Issue 7A is so important. Here is an explanation of the different phases of the project. Critically, Phase 1 service is already paid for and underway. However, Phase 2 will be funded by 7A which can expand both the scope of the system and the number of trips provided per day.

As far as why we need better train infrastructure, here are my main interests:

  • Continue to provide better, non-highway transportation that connects Colorado’s greatest economic centers.
  • Invest in larger, people-mover type infrastructure. A heavy rail train moving 300-500 people at a time is a solid investment, significantly more than a single bus or light rail train in our urban areas.
  • Enabling and fostering the benefits of denser housing and business development. Expanding on existing hubs like Union Station and Boulder Junction, as well as giving incentives for more transit-oriented development, will be a boon for our cities.
  • Continuing to improve access all-electric transportation options, and reducing our reliance on automobiles for long-distance travel (“Front Range Passenger Rail could deliver major transportation and environmental benefits” — SWEEP)

Boulder has paid significant money for a train. Why should I support 7A?

As part of Phase 1 for Front Range Passenger Rail, Boulder is getting its train! That’s already paid for in combination with the Regional Transportation District (RTD):

The Front Range Passenger Rail District says it already has enough funds to create three round trips a day between Denver and Fort Collins in three years, with the route heading northwest to Boulder and Longmont on the way to Larimer County. 

The $332 million limited phase cost would come from a state transportation innovation fund built up by various fees, and RTD money set aside for FasTracks rail lines north of Denver that were never built. The state and RTD would split the $30 million to $36 million annual operating costs for that phase.

Ballot Issue 7A: Front Range voters will weigh sales tax hike to pay for passenger train service — Colorado Sun

However, the biggest risk to Front Range Passenger Rail’s initial implementation is that it will be underwhelming. Only running three trains per day, with minimal station investment, and not extending the entire length of the proposed system. Voting Yes on 7A will ensure the system is adequately funded over the long term and can bring additional revenue and matching grants to expand the system beyond the initial phase.

What happens if 7A fails?

If Issue 7A fails, we’re probably stuck with underwhelming train service, at best. The other issues is that it may jeopardize getting future grants to improve service, such as a $66 million federal grant in 2024 used to improve service between Broomfield and Westminster. It’s not clear what the path forward might be if Issue 7A fails.

How would 7A benefit Boulder and Denver specifically?

Here's a helpful map about where the train would intersect into Boulder. The station will be placed in Boulder Junction, which is a transit-first neighborhood in East Boulder that continues to improve year-over-year. The transit aspect of the neighborhood took a hit since COVID-19 caused the area to lose significant bus service in 2020, which has slowly been improving.

Boulder has pretty excellent transit service along the US 36 / Broadway corridor, but its service to East Boulder is lacking. Providing a long-term boost for residents and businesses in East Boulder could be a solid improvement to make Boulder transit less one-dimensional.

Denver is already well-served as the best transit hub in Colorado, but things can still get better. As an anchor to regional train service to Boulder, Fort Collins, Colorado Springs and Pueblo, Denver benefits from a strengthening region, all connected through Denver Union Station.

I’m also excited to see Colorado sports teams stepping up to endorse Issue 7A, as Denver has a significant regional draw to sporting events which are often difficult to reach from outside of Denver other than by automobile currently.
